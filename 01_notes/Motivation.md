## Theoretical gap: Weaponized interdependence only studies demand-side

Farrell and Newman (2019) launched the weaponized interdependence research program. Their insight: states controlling concentrated nodes in global economic networks can coerce others. The entire empirical literature since then has studied **demand-side** weaponization — states that control who can *buy* or *access* technology, finance, or platforms:

- U.S. controls semiconductor technology (Bown, 2020) — demand-side
- U.S. controls SWIFT/dollar clearing (Farrell and Newman, 2023) — demand-side
- U.S. controls app stores and cloud infrastructure — demand-side

**Nobody has systematically studied supply-side weaponization** — states that control who can *obtain* raw materials. China's critical mineral controls are the first large-scale, multi-mineral, systematic exercise of supply-side coercion in modern trade history.

This matters because supply-side and demand-side weaponization have structurally different properties:

| Property | Demand-side (U.S. semis) | Supply-side (China minerals) |
|----------|--------------------------|------------------------------|
| Chokepoint type | Technology, finance, platforms | Geological resources, processing capacity |
| Circumvention | Develop alternative technology (costly but feasible) | Develop alternative mines (10-15 years, geology-constrained) |
| Durability | Technology evolves, controls become obsolete | Deposits don't move, dominance is stickier |
| Reversibility | Lift export controls → supply resumes immediately | Lift export controls → but processing capacity takes years to build elsewhere |
| Signal | "You cannot buy from us" | "You cannot obtain the raw material regardless of who processes it" |

## Empirical gap: No causal estimates exist

The policy world has noticed China's mineral controls. The IEA, EU, USGS, think tanks (Cato, CSIS, ECIPE) have all published commentary. But the academic literature has not produced a single paper with **causal estimates** of the trade flow or price effects. Everything is either:

- **Qualitative commentary** (Sanderson 2023, Bacchus 2024) — strategic analysis, no causal identification
- **Scenario modeling** (IEA 2023, DOE 2024) — forward-looking projections, not retrospective causal estimates
- **Case studies** (2010 rare earth dispute) — one event, no systematic comparison

This paper provides the first causal estimates using quasi-experimental methods (shift-share DiD, event study). That is the empirical contribution.

## Policy urgency

The window for this research is narrow and the policy need is real:

- **EU Critical Raw Materials Act** (2023) — sets targets for domestic sourcing, but needs evidence on *how bad the disruption is* to calibrate ambition
- **U.S. Critical Minerals Strategy** (2024) — same
- **Japan, South Korea, India** — all developing mineral security strategies, need evidence
- **China itself** — needs to calibrate how aggressively to use export controls (too aggressive → accelerates diversification → erodes leverage)

If diversification investment scales up in response to these controls, the window to measure their effects closes — because the counterfactual (no diversification) disappears. This is the moment to study it.

## Why not just study the 2010 rare earth dispute?

The 2010 China-Japan rare earth dispute is the only precedent. Several reasons it's insufficient:

1. **One mineral, one target** — rare earths to Japan only. The 2023-2025 controls cover 5 mineral categories and target all importers globally
2. **Short duration** — the 2010 restriction lasted weeks. The 2023+ controls are indefinite licensing regimes
3. **Different legal basis** — 2010 was ad hoc. 2023-2025 controls are grounded in China's Export Control Law, making them more institutionalized
4. **Different geopolitical context** — 2010 was a bilateral territorial dispute. 2023-2025 is embedded in systemic US-China technology competition

## Connection to existing vault research

This project is the supply-side mirror of [[chip-war-index/_Index|Chip War Index]], which tracks demand-side weaponization in semiconductors. Together they would provide a complete picture of weaponized interdependence in US-China technology competition.
