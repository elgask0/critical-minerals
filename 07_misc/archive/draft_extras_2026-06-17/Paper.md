# Differential Effectiveness in Supply-Side Weaponization: Causal Evidence from China's Critical Mineral Export Controls

## Abstract

Do export controls work when the coercing state dominates the market? I study China's 2023-2025 export controls on gallium, germanium, and graphite — the first systematic exercise of supply-side weaponized interdependence in modern trade history. Using UN Comtrade monthly data (2019-2024), Bloomberg/Wind domestic price data, and two complementary empirical strategies — interrupted time series and triple-difference — I document **differential effectiveness** across minerals. Graphite controls caused a statistically significant 48% decline in China's export volume (p<0.001, flat pre-trend), confirmed by a 59% relative decline in triple-difference estimation against placebo HS codes (p<0.001) and a domestic price decline of 19.4%. A month-by-month analysis of China's exports reveals a textbook anticipation-implementation sequence: a +101% stockpiling surge in the month between announcement and enforcement (November 2023), followed by an 84% implementation cliff and sustained 39% decline over 12 months. Among high-exposure graphite importers, 79% (by volume) could not substitute toward alternative suppliers — non-China imports also fell, indicating market-wide supply disruption rather than trade redirection. Gallium/germanium controls produced no detectable reduction — exports continued their pre-existing exponential growth trajectory (+3.7%/month, p<0.001), domestic prices rose (+27.7%), and post-control exports surged to 167% above baseline in the three months after licensing began, consistent with porous identity-based controls and a commitment problem under extreme monopoly. The differential effectiveness is consistent with three complementary mechanisms: control design (cumulative-threshold vs. identity-based), market structure (intermediate monopoly vs. extreme monopoly creating a commitment problem), and pre-treatment demand conditions (flat vs. booming). These findings suggest the relationship between market dominance and coercive leverage may be non-monotonic, with implications for both weaponized interdependence theory and diversification policy.

**JEL Codes**: F13, F51, Q37, C23

**Keywords**: Export controls, critical minerals, weaponized interdependence, China, difference-in-differences, interrupted time series

## 1. Introduction

When a state controls a concentrated node in global economic networks, can it coerce others? Farrell and Newman's (2019) theory of weaponized interdependence argues yes — network centrality confers coercive leverage. But the empirical literature has focused almost exclusively on **demand-side** weaponization: states that control who can buy (U.S. semiconductor export controls, SWIFT exclusion). China's 2023-2025 export controls on critical minerals — gallium, germanium, and graphite — represent the first large-scale exercise of **supply-side** weaponization: states that control who can obtain raw materials.

This distinction matters because supply-side and demand-side weaponization have structurally different properties. Demand-side chokepoints (technology, finance) can be circumvented through innovation, albeit at a cost. Supply-side chokepoints (geological deposits, processing infrastructure) require 10-15 years for new mines to reach production and face binding geological constraints. If supply-side weaponization works differently — or does not work at all — the theory of weaponized interdependence is incomplete.

This paper provides the first causal estimates of China's critical mineral export controls using quasi-experimental methods. I find that controls are not uniformly effective: graphite controls caused a statistically significant 48% decline in China's export volume (p<0.001, flat pre-trend), while gallium/germanium controls produced no detectable reduction — exports continued their pre-existing exponential growth trajectory (+3.7% per month driven by semiconductor demand). This **differential effectiveness** is the paper's central empirical finding.

I use two primary identification strategies. First, **interrupted time series** on China's export volumes exploits the abrupt policy implementation in markets with precisely measured pre-treatment trends. Second, **triple-difference** compares controlled HS codes against placebo HS codes, controlling for reporter, period, and product fixed effects. I supplement these with **domestic price event studies** from Bloomberg/Wind data, **announcement-implementation timing analysis** exploiting the one-to-two-month gaps between policy announcement and enforcement, and **substitution analysis** examining whether importers redirected trade toward alternative suppliers.

The paper makes three contributions. **Empirically**, it provides the first causal estimates of China's critical mineral export controls — an urgent policy question given the EU Critical Raw Materials Act (2023) and parallel diversification efforts by Japan, South Korea, and India. **Methodologically**, it demonstrates that ITS is the preferred identification strategy when the treated unit is a single dominant exporter with flat pre-trends, while bilateral panel designs are underpowered in thin markets with limited cross-sectional variation. **Theoretically**, the differential effectiveness is consistent with three complementary mechanisms — control design, market structure, and pre-treatment demand conditions — that together suggest the relationship between market dominance and coercive leverage may be non-monotonic.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 provides institutional background on the policy episodes and mineral market structure. Section 3 develops a theoretical framework and testable hypotheses. Section 4 describes the data and measurement. Section 5 presents the empirical strategy. Section 6 reports the main results. Section 7 discusses robustness. Section 8 addresses limitations. Section 9 concludes with policy implications.

## 2. Institutional Background

### 2.1 The Policy Episodes

Between July 2023 and April 2025, China imposed export licensing requirements on five critical mineral categories under the Export Control Law (effective December 1, 2020). Each was announced by the Ministry of Commerce (MoFCOM) and General Administration of Customs (GAC), citing "national security."

**Episode 1: Gallium and Germanium (August 2023).** On July 3, 2023, MoFCOM and GAC issued Announcement No. 23 of 2023, imposing export licensing on gallium and germanium — covering metallic forms, compounds, epitaxial substrates, wafers, and scrap. The announcement's title verb — 实施 (shíshī, "implement") — signaled **new controls** on previously unrestricted items. The controls took effect August 1, 2023. Affected HS codes include 811292 (gallium and germanium, bundled with indium, vanadium, and niobium) and 282560 (germanium dioxide, bundled with zirconium dioxide). No purity or form thresholds were specified — controls are identity-based: if the chemical composition matches, the product is controlled. MoFCOM confirmed at a November 30, 2023 press conference that export licenses had been approved for some applications, stating that the controls "are not an export ban."

**Episode 2: Graphite (December 2023).** On October 20, 2023, MoFCOM and GAC issued Announcement No. 39 of 2023, imposing export licensing on graphite products effective December 1, 2023. The title verb — 优化调整 (yōuhuà tiáozhěng, "optimize and adjust") — signaled **restructuring** of existing controls, not a new restriction. The legal predecessor is Announcement No. 50 of 2006, which subjected certain graphite products to export licensing for 17 years. No. 39 repealed No. 50 and replaced it with a narrower scope. Paragraph 2 of No. 39 explicitly removes all items listed in the 2006 announcement that do not meet the new cumulative thresholds, meaning fewer items are controlled under the new regime.

The controls are threshold-based: synthetic graphite exceeding >99.9% purity AND >30 MPa flexural strength AND >1.73 g/cm³ density; natural flake graphite, spherical graphite, and expanded graphite. Crucially, artificial graphite powder, reinforced packing, and gaskets are explicitly excluded per MoFCOM's own FAQ. This is a nuanced policy that exempts lower-value downstream products while controlling strategic inputs.

**Episode 3: Antimony (September 2024).** On August 15, 2024, MoFCOM and GAC announced controls on antimony metal, trioxide, ores/concentrates, and processing equipment, effective September 15, 2024. Antimony is imported more intensively by China than exported (China is a net importer of antimony ores), making export controls potentially self-defeating — China restricts exports of processed antimony but simultaneously depends on imported feedstock.

**Episode 4: Rare Earth Elements (April 2025).** The most recent episode, announced April 4, 2025, covers samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, yttrium, and scandium compounds; REE metals and alloys; and NdFeB magnets. This episode is excluded from the main analysis because insufficient post-treatment trade data is available at the time of writing.

### 2.2 Market Structure

China's dominance across these minerals is substantial but varies significantly:

| Mineral | China's global share | Market size | Byproduct status |
|---------|---------------------|-------------|------------------|
| Gallium | ~98% mining and refining | ~$400M/year | Byproduct of alumina refining |
| Germanium | ~68% refining | ~$250M/year | Byproduct of zinc smelting |
| Graphite | ~65% mining, ~90% spherical graphite processing | ~$20B/year | Primary mining |
| Antimony | ~48% mining, ~70% processing | ~$2B/year | Primary mining (declining reserves) |
| REE | ~70% mining, ~85-90% processing | ~$10B/year | Mixed (primary + byproduct) |

The concentration in processing is higher than in mining because China invested decades of industrial policy — state investment, export rebates, below-market pricing — that drove competitors out of business (Mancheri, 2015). For gallium in particular, the byproduct economics create a structural barrier: gallium is recovered from bauxite refining for aluminum, and China processes most of the world's bauxite into alumina, making gallium recovery economical at scale (Frenzel et al., 2017). New entrants would need primary aluminum capacity first, creating a chicken-and-egg problem.

### 2.3 Geopolitical Context

The controls did not emerge in isolation. They form part of a tit-for-tat escalation with U.S. semiconductor export controls: October 2022 (U.S. restricts advanced chip equipment to China) → July 2023 (China responds with Ga/Ge controls) → October 2023 (U.S. expands semiconductor controls) → October 2023 (China responds with graphite controls within one week). This pattern supports a "weaponized interdependence in reverse" framing: China uses supply-side leverage as a symmetric response to U.S. demand-side leverage.

## 3. Theoretical Framework and Hypotheses

### 3.1 Supply-Side Weaponization

Farrell and Newman (2019) argue that states controlling concentrated nodes in global networks can exercise coercive leverage. Their framework emphasizes **network structure** (hub-and-spoke concentration creates chokepoints) and **asymmetric dependence** (the target cannot easily exit the relationship). Applied to supply-side weaponization, the prediction is straightforward: the greater China's market share in a mineral, the greater its coercive leverage, and the more effective its export controls should be.

This implies a **monotonic relationship** between market dominance and coercive effectiveness: China should have the most leverage in gallium (98% share), followed by germanium (68%), graphite (65%), and antimony (48%). Under this framework, gallium controls should be the most effective.

### 3.2 The Commitment Problem in Extreme Monopoly

I challenge the monotonicity assumption by introducing a **commitment problem** specific to supply-side weaponization under extreme monopoly. When a state controls >95% of a market, its threat to restrict supply faces a credibility problem for three reasons:

1. **Self-harm**: The state bears nearly all the cost of reduced exports — lost revenue, idle processing capacity, unemployment in extraction regions. For gallium ($400M market), a complete ban costs China ~$400M/year in direct export revenue.

2. **No substitute suppliers**: The target cannot source from alternative producers (none exist at scale). But this also means the coercing state cannot plausibly threaten to permanently cut off supply — doing so would destroy its own export industry.

3. **License allocation solves nothing**: With no alternative suppliers, approved export licenses do not create competitive pressure. The licensing system merely creates bureaucratic costs that the monopolist can circumvent by approving licenses — which MoFCOM did, as confirmed in their November 2023 press conference.

At intermediate dominance levels (60-90%, as with graphite), the commitment problem is weaker: there are enough alternative suppliers to make the threat of restricted supply credible, but not enough to fully replace Chinese supply. The coercing state can impose costs on targets without fully destroying its own export market.

### 3.3 Empirical Hypotheses and Mechanism Framework

The differential effectiveness documented in this paper could arise from three complementary mechanisms. Because the three mechanisms are perfectly correlated across the two clean treatment cases (graphite vs. Ga/Ge), they cannot be separately identified empirically. I therefore frame them as an interpretive framework and use within-case variation to assess consistency.

**H1 (Differential effectiveness)**: Graphite export controls caused a statistically significant reduction in China's export volume, while gallium/germanium controls did not. This is directly testable.

Three mechanisms are consistent with the predicted differential effectiveness:

**M1 (Control design)**: Controls designed as cumulative-threshold restrictions (graphite) are more enforceable than identity-based controls (Ga/Ge), because thresholds create an administrable bright line while identity-based controls on ubiquitous chemicals are inherently porous.

**M2 (Market structure and commitment problem)**: Extreme monopoly (>95% share, as with gallium) reduces coercive effectiveness because the monopolist cannot credibly threaten to cut off supply — doing so would destroy its own industry. At intermediate dominance (60-90%, as with graphite), the commitment problem is weaker and the coercive threat is credible.

**M3 (Pre-treatment demand conditions)**: Export controls imposed during periods of rapidly growing demand (Ga/Ge in a semiconductor boom) are less effective because the demand surge masks any supply restriction.

While these mechanisms cannot be separately identified with N=2 treatment cases, they generate **testable implications within each case** that can be assessed with the available data:

- If M1 (porous design) matters: Ga/Ge should show evidence of trade rerouting through third countries; graphite should not
- If M2 (commitment problem) matters: Importers should treat Ga/Ge controls as not credible (no anticipatory stockpiling) but graphite controls as credible (stockpiling before implementation); the monopolist should approve Ga/Ge licenses (confirmed by MoFCOM press conference)
- If M3 (demand conditions) matters: Ga/Ge domestic prices should rise (demand overwhelms restriction); graphite domestic prices should fall (binding restriction creates domestic oversupply)
- If M2 (commitment problem) matters: the timing of effects should differ — binding controls (graphite) should produce effects at implementation (not announcement), with anticipatory stockpiling between announcement and enforcement; non-binding controls (Ga/Ge) should produce only a brief announcement dip followed by recovery
- If M3 (demand inelasticity) matters for graphite: importers should be unable to substitute away from China toward alternative suppliers, and non-China imports should also fall (market-wide disruption rather than trade redirection)

## 4. Data

### 4.1 Sources

I use monthly UN Comtrade data accessed via the UN Comtrade API (v1). The data covers January 2019 through December 2024 (72 months, 6 pre-treatment years). For China's export volumes, I download China-as-reporter flows for all treated HS codes. For importer-side analysis, I download bilateral import flows with China as partner for 55 reporter countries representing >95% of global imports of the affected products.

Domestic Chinese price data comes from Bloomberg/Wind Financial Terminal, covering daily spot and average prices for gallium (≥99.99%, CNY/kg), germanium ingot (50Ω/cm, CNY/kg), and natural flake graphite (CNY/ton), aggregated to monthly frequency. All three series have complete coverage for 2019-2024 (72/72 months).

The reporter sample covers all countries that report monthly Comtrade data and imported the affected products from China during the sample period: 31 (Azerbaijan) through 842 (United States), including all OECD members, major emerging economies, and key mineral processing hubs (Vietnam, Malaysia, Thailand).

### 4.3 Measurement and Aggregation

Raw Comtrade data contains duplicate rows because customs authorities report under multiple customs regimes (normal trade, processing trade, etc.). I aggregate by (reporterCode, period) for each HS code, summing primary trade values across customs codes.

The dependent variable is $\ln(1 + \text{Value}_{rpt})$, where $\text{Value}_{rpt}$ is the primary trade value (USD) of reporter $r$'s imports from China of product $p$ in period $t$. The inverse hyperbolic sine transformation is used as robustness.

## 5. Empirical Strategy

### 5.1 Interrupted Time Series (Primary)

The primary identification strategy exploits the abrupt policy implementation in markets where China is the dominant exporter. I model China's total export volume of product $p$ as:

$$\ln(1 + Exports_{pt}) = \alpha + \beta_1 \cdot t + \beta_2 \cdot Post_t + \varepsilon_t$$

where $t$ is a linear time trend (months since January 2019), $Post_t = 1$ for months after the control effective date, and $\beta_2$ is the treatment effect (level shift at implementation). This specification is valid when the pre-treatment trend is linear and the treatment is unanticipated.

ITS is the preferred specification for two reasons. First, when China dominates the market, there is no meaningful control group of non-Chinese exporters — for gallium (98% market share), the 2% produced elsewhere cannot form a synthetic control. Second, ITS transparently documents pre-treatment trends, which is essential for distinguishing treatment effects from trend continuation.

**Graphite is the ideal case** for ITS: pre-treatment trend is approximately zero (+0.13%/month, p=0.53, $R^2$=0.001). The pre-treatment series is remarkably flat — China's monthly exports oscillate around $28M with no systematic trend for 59 months before controls. Any post-treatment deviation from zero can plausibly be attributed to the controls. This is the strongest causal claim in the paper: flat pre-trend, sharp structural break at implementation, and no obvious confound.

**Gallium/germanium is the contrast case**: pre-treatment trend is +3.7%/month (p<0.001, $R^2$=0.463) driven by exponential semiconductor demand growth. China's Ga/Ge exports grew from approximately $45M in 2019 to $87M in the first half of 2023 (the last full pre-treatment semester) — a near-doubling driven by 5G deployment and semiconductor demand. Against this trend, a post-treatment level shift of plausible magnitude cannot be identified as statistically significant. ITS correctly identifies that controls cannot be distinguished from trend continuation.

### 5.2 Triple-Difference

To address the concern that global shocks coincident with the treatment dates affect treated and control products differently, I pool treated HS codes with placebo HS codes and estimate:

$$\ln(1 + M_{rpht}) = \alpha_r + \gamma_t + \eta_h + \beta \cdot (Treated_h \times Post_t) + \varepsilon_{rpht}$$

where $\alpha_r$ are reporter fixed effects, $\gamma_t$ are period fixed effects (absorbing global time shocks), $\eta_h$ are HS-code fixed effects (essential for valid inference — without them, level differences between treated and placebo products are confounded with treatment effects), and $\beta$ captures the differential post-treatment change for treated relative to control HS codes. The interaction $Treated_h \times Post_t$ is identified because it varies across both HS codes (treated vs. placebo) and time (pre vs. post).

## 6. Results

### 6.1 Interrupted Time Series: The Core Finding

Table 1 presents ITS results for the two primary HS codes.

**Table 1: Interrupted Time Series — China Export Volume**

| HS Code | Product | Pre-trend | p | $R^2_{pre}$ | Level shift | p | % change |
|---------|---------|-----------|---|-------------|------------|---|----------|
| 2504 | Natural graphite | +0.0013/mo | 0.53 | 0.001 | **-0.6495** | **0.0001** | **-47.8%** |
| 811292 | Ga/Ge/In/V/Nb | +0.0366/mo | <0.0001 | 0.463 | +0.1114 | 0.68 | +11.8% |

**Graphite (HS 2504)**. The pre-treatment trend is essentially flat (+0.13%/month, p=0.53). China's monthly exports oscillated around $28M with no systematic trend for 59 months before controls. At the December 2023 implementation date, exports experienced a level shift of -0.6495 log points — a 47.8% decline (p<0.0001). November 2023 shows a substantial spike ($51.9M vs. typical $28M), consistent with last-minute stockpiling by importers anticipating the controls. December 2023 exports collapse to $4.1M. Exports partially recover over subsequent months but remain significantly below pre-treatment levels.

The flat pre-trend (R²=0.001) is critical for identification. Unlike most policy evaluations where the counterfactual must be estimated from noisy data, the graphite pre-treatment trend is so precisely estimated as zero that any post-treatment deviation is directly interpretable as causal.

**Gallium/Germanium (HS 811292)**. The pre-treatment trend is +3.7%/month (p<0.0001, R²=0.463), driven by exponential semiconductor demand growth. China's Ga/Ge exports grew from approximately $45M in 2019 to $87M in the last full pre-treatment semester (2023H1). The post-treatment level shift of +0.11 log points is not statistically significant (p=0.68). Exports continued their upward trajectory after August 2023.

**The ITS is inherently underpowered for Ga/Ge.** With a pre-trend R² of 0.463, the trend explains nearly half the variation in the pre-treatment data. Any treatment effect must be detected as a deviation from this steep trend — a deviation of even 30-50% would be statistically indistinguishable from trend continuation. This is not a methodological failure; it is the correct conclusion. Controls imposed during a period of exponential demand growth cannot be distinguished from trend continuation using ITS. The Ga/Ge null is therefore a statement about identifiability, not necessarily about the absence of any effect.

### 6.2 Triple-Difference

The triple-difference pools treated HS codes with placebo HS codes (2818 alumina, 8111 manganese, 2811 inorganic acids), controlling for reporter, period, and HS code fixed effects. HS FE are essential: without them, level differences between treated and placebo products (treated codes have much higher trade volumes) are confounded with treatment effects, producing spurious results. The placebos are drawn from HS chapters 28 and 81, which contain the treated codes 811292 and 282560. Graphite (Chapter 25) lacks a same-chapter placebo; the comparison is against minerals from adjacent chapters facing similar global demand conditions.

**Table 2: Triple-Difference (reporter + period + HS FE)**

| Specification | $\beta$ | p-value | % change |
|---------------|---------|---------|----------|
| Graphite (2504) | **-0.8906** | **<0.0001** | **-59.0%** |
| Ga/Ge (811292) | +0.0217 | 0.93 | +2.2% |

The triple-diff confirms the ITS findings. Graphite shows a highly significant relative decline of 59.0% (p<0.0001) compared to placebo HS codes — the export decline was specific to the controlled product, not a general decline in mineral imports. Ga/Ge shows no differential change (p=0.93), confirming that Ga/Ge imports continued growing alongside placebos.

### 6.3 Domestic Price Evidence

Domestic Chinese prices provide an independent test: if controls bind, restricted exports create domestic oversupply and prices should fall.

**Table 3: Domestic Price ITS (Bloomberg/Wind, 2019-2024)**

| Mineral | Raw Δ | ITS shift | p | Pre-trend R² | Consistent with binding controls? |
|---------|-------|-----------|---|-------------|----------------------------------|
| Graphite (CNY/ton) | **-19.4%** | -32.6% | <0.0001 | 0.240 | **Yes: domestic oversupply** |
| Gallium (CNY/kg) | **+27.7%** | -32.1% (below trend) | 0.0002 | 0.603 | **No: prices rose** |
| Indium placebo (CNY/kg) | — | +34.4% | <0.0001 | — | **No: broad metals inflation** |

Graphite prices fell from CNY 4,384 to 3,534/ton (-19.4% raw) after controls, consistent with domestic oversupply from restricted exports. Gallium prices rose from CNY 1,718 to 2,195/kg (+27.7% raw) despite controls. The ITS reports -32.1% because it subtracts the strong pre-trend (R²=0.603), but the raw price went up — inconsistent with a binding restriction. The indium placebo confirms broad metals inflation: prices rose +34% at both treatment dates (p<0.0001), undermining any interpretation of Ga/Ge price changes as control-specific.

### 6.4 Mechanism Tests

The differential effectiveness is established by Sections 6.1-6.3. This section tests whether within-case variation is consistent with the three mechanisms proposed in Section 3.3.

**Test 1: Announcement vs. Implementation Timing (Consistent with M2 — Commitment Problem)**

If controls are credible, effects should materialize at implementation (not announcement), with anticipatory stockpiling in the interim. If controls are not credible, announcement may produce a brief uncertainty dip followed by rapid normalization. China's own export data — the cleanest signal — provides month-by-month evidence.

**Table 4: China Export Dynamics Around Announcement and Implementation**

| Phase | Graphite | vs baseline | Gallium | vs baseline |
|-------|----------|-------------|---------|-------------|
| 12-mo baseline | $25.8M/mo | — | $17.0M/mo | — |
| Announcement month | $18.7M | -28% | $12.1M | -28% |
| Anticipation window | **$51.9M** | **+101%** | — | — |
| Implementation month | **$4.1M** | **-84%** | $10.4M | -39% |
| Post 1-3 months | $12.7M | -51% | **$45.3M** | **+167%** |
| Post 4-6 months | $19.9M | -23% | $23.9M | +41% |
| Post 7-12 months | $17.9M | -31% | $17.0M | +1% |
| 12-mo post mean | **$15.8M** | **-39%** | **$25.8M** | **+52%** |

*Notes: Baseline = 12 months before announcement. Graphite: announced Oct 20, 2023, implemented Dec 1, 2023 (anticipation window = November). Gallium: announced Jul 3, 2023, implemented Aug 1, 2023 (no anticipation window).*

**Graphite** exhibits a textbook anticipation-implementation sequence. The October 2023 announcement produces a modest 28% decline — consistent with initial uncertainty. Then November 2023 exports surge to $51.9M, double the baseline — importers rush-ordered shipments before controls took effect. December 2023 (implementation) produces a cliff: exports collapse to $4.1M (-84%). The 12-month post-implementation mean remains 39% below baseline, confirming sustained binding. The anticipation surge is direct behavioral evidence that market actors treated graphite controls as credible: they incurred real costs (premium pricing, expedited shipping) to secure supply before enforcement.

**Gallium** shows the opposite pattern. The July 2023 announcement and August 2023 implementation both produce modest dips (-28%, -39%), consistent with temporary bureaucratic uncertainty as the licensing system came online. But exports then surge to $45.3M/month in September-November 2023 — 167% above baseline and far exceeding any pre-treatment level. China exported more gallium in the three months after controls than in any comparable pre-treatment period. The 12-month post-implementation mean is +52% above baseline. There is no anticipation surge, no implementation cliff, and no sustained reduction — importers correctly anticipated that controls would not restrict supply.

The asymmetry is consistent with M2: importers treated graphite controls as a credible threat (incurred real stockpiling costs) but Ga/Ge controls as not credible (no stockpiling, exports surged immediately after licensing began). This is direct behavioral evidence of the commitment problem operating through market expectations.

**Test 2: Price Direction (Consistent with M3 — Demand Conditions)**

Graphite domestic prices fell after controls (-19.4%), consistent with binding restrictions creating domestic oversupply. Gallium domestic prices rose (+27.7%), consistent with demand growth overwhelming any supply restriction. The pre-trend R² of 0.603 for gallium prices confirms the demand boom was already driving prices up before controls; after controls, demand continued to dominate.

**Test 3: Trade Rerouting Through Hong Kong (Consistent with M1 — Porous Controls)**

Hong Kong is a classic transshipment hub. If Ga/Ge controls are porous (identity-based, M1), we should observe trade rerouting through Hong Kong after controls. If graphite controls are strict (threshold-based), we should not.

| Mineral | HK imports from China: Pre → Post | HK total imports: Pre → Post |
|---------|----------------------------------|------------------------------|
| Graphite | -58.6% | -70.4% |
| Ga/Ge | **+170.8%** | **+276.8%** |

Hong Kong's Ga/Ge imports from China surged by 171% after controls, and total Ga/Ge imports (from all partners) surged by 277%. This is consistent with trade rerouting or transshipment through Hong Kong to circumvent export licensing. For graphite, Hong Kong imports declined in line with the overall market decline — no rerouting. This pattern is consistent with M1: identity-based Ga/Ge controls are porous enough to permit rerouting through intermediaries, while threshold-based graphite controls are not.

**Test 4: Market Disruption and Substitution Failure (Consistent with M3 — Demand Inelasticity)**

If graphite controls merely redirected trade away from China, importers would substitute toward alternative suppliers and total (non-China) imports would rise. If controls reduced total market supply — because alternative sources cannot expand at scale — non-China imports should also fall. This distinction matters for assessing the economic magnitude of controls: trade redirection affects bilateral flows; market disruption affects global supply.

I examine the 13 reporters with pre-treatment China share exceeding 20% — the importers most exposed to controls and most likely to seek alternative suppliers.

| Reporter | China share | China imports Δ | Non-China imports Δ | Pattern |
|----------|-------------|----------------|--------------------|----------|

| Japan (392) | 46% | -34% | -29% | Both fell |

| Egypt (818) | 47% | -59% | -49% | Both fell |

The dominant pattern is **market disruption, not substitution**. Among the five largest high-exposure graphite importers (Korea, Japan, Kazakhstan, Egypt, Hong Kong), all five saw non-China imports fall alongside China imports. Weighted by pre-treatment China import volume, 79% of high-exposure trade experienced both China and non-China declines. Only two smaller importers (Turkey, India) successfully substituted.

This finding is economically significant. It indicates that graphite controls did not merely redirect bilateral trade — they reduced the total available supply to importers. Alternative graphite producers (Mozambique, Brazil, Madagascar) could not expand output to absorb the shortfall, consistent with the geological and processing constraints on graphite supply (Section 2.2). The result is consistent with demand inelasticity (M3): when a dominant supplier restricts output in a market with limited alternatives, the restriction propagates through the entire supply chain, not just the bilateral relationship.

For gallium, the substitution question is moot: China's gallium exports increased after controls (Table 4), so there is no supply shock from which to substitute.

**Summary**: Five mechanism tests produce results consistent with the proposed framework. The announcement-implementation timing analysis shows that graphite produced anticipation stockpiling followed by a sustained implementation cliff, while gallium produced only a brief uncertainty dip followed by record exports — direct behavioral evidence of differential credibility consistent with the commitment problem (M2). The price direction supports the demand conditions mechanism (M3). The Hong Kong rerouting supports the control design mechanism (M1). The substitution failure shows that graphite controls reduced total market supply, not just Chinese bilateral flows — 79% of high-exposure importers (by volume) saw non-China sources dry up alongside Chinese ones, consistent with demand inelasticity in markets with limited alternative suppliers. Together, these tests demonstrate that the differential effectiveness is associated with observable differences in importer expectations, price dynamics, trade routing, and substitution possibilities that are consistent with the proposed mechanisms.

## 7. Robustness

### 7.1 GeO₂ and Artificial Graphite (HS 282560, 3801)

Two additional HS codes provide robustness checks. HS 282560 (GeO₂ bundled with ZrO₂) shows a significant ITS level shift (-25.5%, p=0.004) but has a significant pre-trend (p=0.0003, pre-R²=0.190), weakening the causal interpretation. Its triple-diff coefficient is significant (β=-0.4336, p=0.035, -35.2%), but the contaminated pre-trend still limits causal interpretation. HS 3801 (artificial graphite, partially treated) shows a large decline (-48.8%, p<0.0001) with a strong pre-trend (pre-R²=0.835). Both are reported for completeness but excluded from the main analysis due to these identification concerns.

### 7.2 Shift-Share Design (Underpowered)

A shift-share difference-in-differences exploiting cross-reporter variation in pre-treatment China exposure produces null results for all three minerals (all p > 0.29 with reporter + period fixed effects). This is consistent with the structural limitations of these markets: mean China exposure is 12-15%, concentrated in 5-6 reporters, with 50-67% zero observations. The shift-share design lacks sufficient cross-sectional variation to detect effects in these thin markets. The null is informative — it confirms that bilateral panel methods are underpowered for this application, motivating the reliance on ITS and triple-diff.

### 7.3 Stockpiling Anticipation

The graphite series shows clear evidence of stockpiling: November 2023 exports of $51.9M versus a typical $20-34M range. December 2023 exports collapse to $4.1M. The stockpiling spike attenuates the measured treatment effect (some pre-treatment export increase would have been post-treatment demand pulled forward). The "true" treatment effect, accounting for stockpiling, is likely larger than the -48% ITS estimate.

### 7.4 Bundled HS Codes

HS 811292 bundles gallium and germanium with indium, vanadium, and niobium — four other metals not subject to controls. HS 282560 bundles GeO₂ with ZrO₂. This measurement error attenuates the Ga/Ge results toward zero: any control effect on Ga is diluted by uncontrolled In/V/Nb. The null finding is therefore conservative — even with attenuation, no treatment effect is detected.

### 7.5 Alternative Specifications

Results are robust to: (1) inverse hyperbolic sine instead of log transformation; (2) excluding the stockpiling month (November 2023); (3) excluding China itself as a reporter (China reports its own trade flows in the bilateral data); (4) using 24-month instead of 60-month pre-treatment window for share computation.

## 8. Discussion and Limitations

### 8.1 Internal Validity

The graphite ITS result is internally valid under the assumption of no confound coincident with December 2023 that would independently cause a 48% decline in China's natural graphite exports. The flat pre-trend makes this assumption plausible but not certain. Potential confounds include:

- **EV battery demand shift**: A simultaneous decline in battery demand could reduce graphite exports. However, global EV sales continued growing in 2023-2024.
- **China's environmental inspections**: Periodic environmental crackdowns on graphite mining (especially in Shandong and Heilongjiang) could independently restrict supply. These are typically temporary (weeks to months) and predictable, whereas the December 2023 decline persisted.
- **Concurrent export controls on related products**: The announcement and implementation share the same date, preventing separation.

### 8.2 External Validity and Mechanism Identification

The paper's core finding — differential effectiveness between graphite and Ga/Ge — is robust across three independent methods (ITS, triple-diff, price data). However, the mechanism analysis has important limitations.

The three proposed mechanisms (control design, market structure, demand conditions) are perfectly correlated across the two clean treatment cases: graphite has threshold controls + intermediate monopoly + flat demand; Ga/Ge has identity controls + extreme monopoly + booming demand. This collinearity means the mechanisms cannot be separately identified with N=2. The within-case tests in Section 6.4 (stockpiling, price direction, Hong Kong rerouting) provide evidence consistent with each mechanism but cannot establish independent causal effects.

The non-monotonic relationship between market share and coercive leverage is a theoretical hypothesis consistent with the data, not an empirically established finding. Two data points cannot establish a functional form. The antimony (48% share, September 2024) and REE (70% share, April 2025) controls will provide additional test cases as post-treatment data accumulates.

### 8.3 Data Limitations

Several data constraints affect the analysis:

- **Limited disaggregated Ga/Ge coverage**: Six-digit HS codes bundle controlled metals with uncontrolled ones. Eurostat CN8 data now separate Ga and Ge from In and V for the current EU27, but this remains a regional robustness sample rather than global coverage.
- **Bloomberg price data**: Domestic Chinese price series (gallium, germanium, graphite) from Bloomberg/Wind are incorporated in Section 6.3. All three treated minerals have complete monthly price coverage for 2019-2024. No antimony or GeO₂-specific price series are available.
- **No firm-level data**: MoFCOM's license approval process — the mechanism through which controls operate — operates at the firm level. Firm-level customs data could reveal whether license approvals are concentrated among politically connected firms.

### 8.4 Theoretical Interpretation

The findings are consistent with a non-monotonic relationship between network centrality and coercive leverage (Farrell and Newman, 2019). Extreme centrality may create a commitment problem: the coercing state cannot credibly threaten to disrupt a network it dominates entirely, because doing so would destroy its own position. This hypothesis — that coercive leverage is maximized at intermediate dominance levels — is a theoretical contribution that deserves further testing with additional cases as more data becomes available.

An alternative interpretation is that Ga/Ge controls were never intended to restrict supply — they were "signaling controls" designed to demonstrate China's capacity for retaliation without actually disrupting markets. Under this interpretation, the differential effectiveness is not a puzzle but a feature: Ga/Ge controls signal capability, graphite controls impose costs. The two serve different strategic purposes. This interpretation is consistent with the MoFCOM press conference framing, which emphasized that the controls were "not an export ban" and that licenses would be granted for legitimate end users.

The signaling interpretation, if correct, has implications for how we evaluate Chinese economic statecraft. It suggests that China calibrates coercive intensity to match the strategic stakes: Ga/Ge controls responded to U.S. semiconductor export controls (symmetric retaliation at the technology level), while graphite controls responded to continued escalation (asymmetric escalation at the raw-material level). This calibration is more sophisticated than the "China is irrational or reckless" narrative common in Western commentary. It also implies that China has internalized the strategic logic of the commitment problem and tailors controls accordingly — consistent with the mechanism-based explanation proposed here.

## 9. Conclusion

China's 2023-2025 critical mineral export controls represent the first systematic exercise of supply-side weaponized interdependence. This paper provides causal evidence that these controls are differentially effective: graphite controls caused a 48% decline in China's export volume (p<0.001), confirmed by a 59% relative decline in triple-difference estimation against placebo HS codes (p<0.001), while gallium/germanium controls produced no detectable effect against a backdrop of exponential semiconductor-driven demand growth. A month-by-month analysis of China's own export records reveals the mechanism: graphite produced an anticipation stockpiling surge (+101%) followed by an implementation cliff (-84%) and sustained 12-month decline (-39%), while gallium produced only a brief bureaucratic pause before exports surged to record highs (+167%) — direct behavioral evidence that market actors correctly anticipated which controls would bind. Domestic price data corroborates the differential finding: graphite prices fell after controls (consistent with domestic oversupply from restricted exports), while gallium and germanium prices rose (consistent with continued demand growth overwhelming any supply restriction). Among high-exposure graphite importers, 79% of trade (by volume) experienced market disruption — both Chinese and non-Chinese supplies contracted simultaneously — indicating that controls reduced total market supply rather than merely redirecting bilateral trade.

The finding carries three implications. **For theory**, the relationship between market dominance and coercive leverage may be non-monotonic — extreme monopoly can undermine coercive credibility, consistent with the commitment problem hypothesis. **For policy**, importing countries should prioritize diversification not in the most concentrated markets (where commitment problems blunt coercive force) but in intermediate-concentration markets where coercive leverage is maximized. **For methodology**, interrupted time series can outperform bilateral panel designs when the treated unit is a single dominant exporter with well-measured pre-treatment trends — a common scenario in trade policy analysis.

China's antimony (September 2024) and rare earth (April 2025) controls provide natural extensions for future research. As post-treatment data accumulates, these cases can test whether the mechanisms identified here — control design, market structure, and pre-treatment conditions — generalize to other minerals. The policy window for this research is narrowing: as importing countries accelerate diversification, the counterfactual of no-diversification will disappear. The contribution of this paper is not only the specific estimates for graphite and Ga/Ge but the analytical framework for evaluating supply-side weaponization more broadly — a framework that will become increasingly important as China and other resource-dominant states consider similar measures.
